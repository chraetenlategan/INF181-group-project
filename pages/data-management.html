Data and information management

The longest page here, and the one worth your time. Everything else in this resource assumes the idea on this page: that a fact captured properly once can be used everywhere, and a fact typed into a cell cannot.

Explain data vs information vs knowledge, using the shop's own examples (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Define data, information and knowledge in plain language — no textbook phrasing the owner would skip past.
  • Use one fact and follow it through all three. Suggested: "12" (data) → "12 metres of 20mm polycop sold to Thandi Mabaso on 11 October" (information) → "plumbing pipe sells in bursts after month-end, so order before the 25th" (knowledge).
  • Make the point that the shop already produces all the data it needs. The failure is not collection, it is retention and structure.
  • Explain why this is not word games: the owner cannot act on data, only on information. Every step in this resource is about closing that gap.

Where to look it up: Your prescribed IS textbook's treatment of the DIKW hierarchy — cite the edition and page.

Explain the structured record and the schema (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Point out that roughly half the record is the envelope. Explain why that is a feature, not waste.
  • Explain what a schema is — the shop's agreed definition of what a customer record contains — and why writing it down once means the form, the checks and the storage can never disagree.
  • Explain the difference between master data (customers, products, suppliers — things that exist) and transaction data (sales, stock movements — things that happened). Master data is corrected; transaction data is never edited, only reversed. Explain why that rule matters for trust.
  • Address the obvious objection: "this looks complicated." Nobody types this. It is produced by filling in a form with eight boxes.

Evidence you can point at: The whole schema is in data/schema.js and drives the capture forms in the demonstration.

Information quality: six questions, one score

"Is the data good?" is too vague to act on. These six questions are not. Each one is a check a system can run by itself, and each one is currently failing somewhere in the shop's records.

Each example is a real failure in the demonstration data set, taken from the migrated spreadsheet.
DimensionThe questionWhat it looks like at Lerato's
AccuracyDoes the value match reality? A screwdriver set priced at R109 that costs R112. Every sale loses R3 and nobody noticed, because no rule checked.
CompletenessIs anything missing that you need? Customer CUS-015: no recorded reason for holding their details, an incomplete phone number — and R12 000 of credit.
ConsistencyDoes the same thing appear the same way everywhere? "Sarah Nel" and "Sara Nel", one mobile number between them. Two records, one person, two credit limits.
TimelinessIs it current enough to decide on? A stock figure last counted in June is not a stock figure in October — it is a memory of one.
ValidityDoes it fit the rules? A VAT number recorded as 47712. A South African VAT number is ten digits. This one cannot be real.
UniquenessIs each real thing stored exactly once? The duplicate above means the shop has effectively granted one person two credit limits without deciding to.

Write the information quality section (350–450 words — this is a marked sub-topic)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Introduce the six dimensions properly. Your textbook will have its own list; use it and cite it, then map it to the table above.
  • Make the cost concrete. Pick the duplicate customer and follow the consequence: two records, one person, a credit limit applied twice, a bad debt that was never authorised.
  • Explain the "cost of poor quality" idea — it is usually cheapest to prevent at capture, more expensive to correct later, most expensive when a decision has already been made on it.
  • Explain why the score on the demonstration is deliberately calculated over master data and not over thousands of routine sales. A score that cannot go down is worse than no score.
  • Be honest: no system reaches 100%, and chasing it is a waste. The useful question is which failures cost money.

Evidence you can point at: The Data quality screen lists every failing record by name, with the rule it broke.

Catching it at the counter, not at month-end

A rule that fires while the customer is still standing there costs thirty seconds. The same error found at month-end costs an afternoon, and found after a decision has been made on it, it costs real money.

Error stops the save

Reserved for things that are definitely wrong and cannot be a deliberate exception.

  • A sale pointing at a product that does not exist
  • A selling price below the cost price
  • "On account" for a cash-only customer
  • Marketing consent ticked with no date recorded
  • A sale dated in the future

Warning asks, then records the answer

The record saves, but the person has to acknowledge it, and the acknowledgement is stored in the envelope.

  • Selling more than the recorded stock on hand
  • A margin under 10%
  • A customer going over their credit limit
  • A mobile number already on file for somebody else
  • A stock adjustment over 50 units

Explain why there are two severities and not one (200–300 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • This is the most quietly important design decision in the whole system, and it is worth a paragraph of its own in the pitch.
  • Shops have genuine exceptions: a damaged item sold cheap, a trusted customer let past their limit, stock that is physically there but was never booked in.
  • A system that refuses every exception gets worked around — staff stop using it, or start entering fiction to make it accept the save. A system that is worked around holds worse data than no system at all.
  • So warnings let the human overrule the machine, and the overrule is recorded. The owner can later ask how often a limit was overridden and by whom.
  • Connect this to the broader point about designing systems for the people who will actually use them — it links forward to the Making it fit page.

Evidence you can point at: In the Till screen, try selling 500 bags of cement. You can, but you have to tick the acknowledgement, and it is stored on the record.

Write the data sources section (250–300 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain why mapping sources comes before choosing software. You cannot integrate what you have not listed.
  • The last row is the serious one. Knowledge that exists only in the owner's head is a risk to the business — if they are ill, on leave, or want to sell the shop one day, it is not transferable.
  • Explain "single source of truth" here: for each fact, decide which place is authoritative. Two places holding a stock figure means they will disagree, and nobody will know which is right.
  • Suggest the practical first exercise: the owner writes this table out for their own shop before talking to any vendor.

Write the reuse section (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • This is where the owner is persuaded, so spend the words. Anchor it in an afternoon they recognise.
  • Make the agreement point: the quote and the invoice cannot disagree, because they are two views of the same records rather than two separately typed documents.
  • Explain "provenance" — each generated document in the demonstration lists the record IDs it was built from, so an argument about an invoice is settled by looking rather than remembering.
  • Mention the compliance by-product: a tax invoice has required particulars under the VAT Act. Getting them onto every invoice automatically is worth money the first time SARS or a VAT-registered customer asks.
  • Verify the current required particulars for a tax invoice against SARS guidance and cite it — do not state the list from memory.

Evidence you can point at: Documents screen. Generate an invoice and read the grey provenance line at the bottom.

Where to look it up: SARS guidance on tax invoices and the VAT Act requirements; the Information Regulator for the POPIA report.

Dashboards: what to put on one, and what to leave off

Most small-business dashboards fail the same way — they show everything that can be counted, so nobody looks at them twice. A dashboard is not a report. It is a short list of things worth interrupting the day for.

The test for every tile on a dashboard

"If this number moved, what would I do differently today?" If there is no answer, the number belongs in a monthly report, not on the daily screen. Most dashboards fail this test on most of their tiles.

Write the dashboards and data visualisation section (350–450 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain the difference between a dashboard (daily, short, action-oriented) and a report (periodic, detailed, for review).
  • Cover the visualisation basics properly, because "data visualisation" is named in the brief: pick the chart for the question, not for decoration. Columns for change over time, horizontal bars when the labels are words, a donut only for share-of-whole with few slices. Say plainly that 3-D and pie charts with fifteen slices communicate nothing.
  • Explain why the demonstration's main chart puts revenue columns and a margin line together: selling more and earning more are different things, and a shop can do the first while losing the second.
  • Cover one honest pitfall each: truncated axes that exaggerate, comparing a part-month against a full month, and averages hiding the thing that matters. The demonstration deliberately fixes the second one — explain how and why.
  • Explain why exceptions (band 3) are the part staff actually use, and that it is a list of names, not a number.

Evidence you can point at: The Today screen is built to this structure. Note the revenue tile compares the first 11 days of this month against the first 11 days of last month, not against a full month.

Where to look it up: A recognised data-visualisation source — Few, Tufte, or your textbook's chapter — cited properly.

Cover, in your own words:

  • Define each measure in one plain sentence — assume the reader has never seen "stock turn" written down.
  • Explain why gross margin matters more than turnover to a shop of this size, with a worked example in rands.

Keeping it: backups, retention and the stolen laptop

The question that decides everything else

If the shop laptop were stolen tonight, what would the business lose? For most small businesses running on spreadsheets, the honest answer is: everything except what is in the invoice book, and nobody would find out until they needed it.

Team writes this author: ____

Write the data protection and retention section (300–400 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Cover backups properly: more than one copy, at least one of them not in the shop, and — the part everyone skips — a restore that has actually been tested. An untested backup is a hope, not a backup.
  • Explain the trade-off between cloud and on-premise honestly for a South African small business: load shedding, data costs, patchy connectivity, versus the risk of the one laptop.
  • Cover retention from both sides: SARS requires business records to be kept for a set period, while POPIA requires personal information not to be kept longer than needed. These pull in opposite directions and the shop needs a written answer. Verify both periods against SARS and the Information Regulator and cite them — do not state them from memory.
  • Cover access: who can see what, and why that is about not asking staff to be careful with screens they never needed, rather than about distrust.
  • Mention the audit trail as the thing that answers "who changed this?" — unanswerable in a shared spreadsheet.

Evidence you can point at: The Audit trail records every change with the old and new value. The Your data screen exports everything to a file the owner can keep.

Where to look it up: SARS record-retention requirements; POPIA sections 14 and 19; the Information Regulator's guidance notes.

What to do on Monday

Nothing on this page needs software to start.

  1. Write down where every fact enters the business

    The seven-row table above, for this shop. One page, half an hour. Everything else depends on it.

  2. Decide the single source of truth for each fact

    For stock, for price, for what a customer owes. One place each. Write down which.

  3. Agree the minimum you will capture for every sale

    Even in the invoice book. Date, who served, what was sold, what it cost, how it was paid.

  4. Count the stock properly once

    Any system you buy will inherit whatever figure you give it. Starting a new system on a June guess means paying for a system that is wrong on day one.

  5. Then, and only then, look at software

    The roadmap sets out the stages and what each costs.
