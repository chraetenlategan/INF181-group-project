AI and decision support

Which decisions a small shop should hand to a system, which it should keep for itself, and how to tell a useful tool from an expensive subscription with the word "AI" on the box.

Almost everything sold as "business intelligence" or "AI" answers one of four questions. They build on each other, and skipping a step does not work.

Which decisions should a system make?

Not all decisions are the same shape. The useful split is how repeatable they are.

TypeWhat it meansAt Lerato'sGive it to a system?
Structured The rule is clear, the inputs are known, the answer is the same every time When to reorder cement. How much VAT on an invoice. Whether this customer is over their credit limit. Yes — automate it
Semi-structured Part of it can be calculated; part needs judgement Which slow line to mark down. Whether to extend a contractor's terms. What to stock for winter. System advises, person decides
Unstructured No rule, no reliable inputs, consequences are strategic Whether to open a second branch. Whether to take on a partner. Whether to sell up. Keep it — but bring better data to it

Write the decision types section (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Define structured, semi-structured and unstructured decisions, citing your textbook.
  • Make the practical point: automate the structured ones because they are boring, repetitive and done badly by tired people at 5pm on a Friday — not because computers are clever.
  • Explain "decision support" properly: the system's job on a semi-structured decision is to put the evidence in front of the person, not to make the call.
  • Give the counter-example: the reorder calculation in the demonstration suggests a quantity. It does not place the order. Explain why that boundary is deliberate.

What is actually useful to a shop this size

Honest assessment, in order of what returns the most for the least.

Worth it now

  • Reorder suggestions — sales rate against supplier lead time. Arithmetic, not AI, and it is the single highest-value calculation in the whole system.
  • Exception lists — what is below reorder, who is over 60 days, which records are failing validation.
  • Margin by product and by customer — reveals which trade accounts are busy rather than profitable.
  • Dead stock and shrinkage — both are the owner's money, and neither is visible on paper.

Later, with clean data

  • Seasonal demand forecasting — genuinely useful for roof paint, geyser elements and building materials, but needs two or three years of trustworthy history.
  • Basket analysis — what sells with what, which drives layout and add-on selling.
  • Customer segmentation — separating contractors from DIY walk-ins properly rather than by impression.
  • Reading supplier invoices automatically — the technology works; the question is whether the volume justifies it.

Be sceptical

  • "AI-powered" anything with no explanation of what it does. Ask what it predicts, from what data, and how often it is wrong.
  • Automated pricing — in a market where relationships and a handshake price matter, this breaks things quietly.
  • Automatic ordering with no human approval. One bad forecast becomes a yard full of stock.
  • Customer chatbots for a shop with one phone line and loyal regulars who would rather speak to Sipho.

Check against POPIA first

  • Anything that profiles customers by behaviour.
  • Anything that uses purchase history for marketing without specific consent.
  • Anything that sends customer data to a third-party service to be analysed — including a general-purpose AI assistant.
  • Automated decisions that affect a person, such as refusing credit. Verify what s.71 requires here and cite it.

Write the honest assessment of AI for a small retailer (350–450 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • This section is where the resource earns credibility with a sceptical client. Do not oversell.
  • Start with the deflation: most of what delivers value to a shop this size is arithmetic done consistently, not machine learning. Say so plainly.
  • Explain what a forecast actually is — an estimate with uncertainty — and that a forecast presented without a sense of how wrong it might be is worse than no forecast, because it invites confident bad decisions.
  • Explain the data requirement honestly: machine learning needs volume, history and consistency. A shop with eighteen months of spreadsheet data of uncertain quality does not have the inputs yet.
  • Cover bias with a retail example, not an abstract one. If the shop never stocked a line, the data shows no demand for it, and a model trained on that data will recommend never stocking it. The data records what the shop did, not what customers wanted.
  • Cover explainability: the owner must be able to ask "why did it suggest that?" and get an answer. A recommendation nobody can explain will be ignored the first time it looks odd — correctly.
  • Give three questions to ask any vendor selling AI, and make them questions a non-technical owner could actually ask out loud.

Where to look it up: Your textbook on decision support systems and business intelligence; any reputable source on forecasting limitations or algorithmic bias.

What the system must never be allowed to decide

The line, stated once

A system should decide things that are repeatable. A person should decide things that are consequential and rare. The failure mode of a small business is not having too little automation — it is automating a judgement call and then not noticing when the circumstances changed.

Write the human judgement section (200–300 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Give concrete examples specific to this shop: extending credit to a long-standing customer going through a hard patch, pricing for a neighbour, deciding whether to keep stocking a line that one loyal customer depends on.
  • Make the point that these decisions have social and relational content that is not in the data and never will be.
  • Connect to the ethics page: a system that quietly refuses credit based on purchase patterns is making a decision about a person's life with no accountability and no right of reply.
  • Close by saying what the system should do with these decisions instead: surface the evidence, record what was decided and by whom, and let the owner be answerable for it.
