Enterprise systems and integration

What the standard pieces of business software actually do, why they stop talking to each other, and what it costs when nobody planned for the joins.

The pieces, in plain language

Every one of these already exists at Lerato's. They are just being done by a person, a book, or nobody at all.

SystemWhat it is forWho does it at Lerato's today
Point of sale (POS) Records the sale as it happens: what, how much, to whom, paid how The duplicate invoice book, plus the Yoco machine for card payments
Inventory management Knows what is on the shelf, what it cost, and when to order more A spreadsheet last reconciled in June, and Thabo's memory

Customer management (CRM) Who the customers are, what they buy, what they owe, what they consented to The exercise book and the owner's memory

Write the enterprise systems overview (300–400 words — marked sub-topic)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Define each system properly in your own words. Define ERP carefully — it is the term most likely to come up in Q&A and the one most often misused.
  • Make the point in the right-hand column explicit: the shop already performs every one of these functions. The question is never "should we have inventory management" but "should it be done by a book or by a system".
  • Explain why a full ERP is usually wrong for a business this size: cost, implementation length, and the fact that it is designed to impose process discipline a four-person shop does not need yet.
  • Explain what a module is, and why buying one product that does two things well beats buying four that each do one thing.
  • Cite your textbook for the definitions, and name specific South African products only where you can verify what they do.

Islands of data

This is the single most common failure in small-business IT, and it is not caused by bad software. It is caused by buying good software one piece at a time, over years, with nobody asking how the pieces will talk.

Write the islands-of-data section (300–400 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain the term data silo / islands of data in plain language.
  • Trace one fact — the price of a bag of cement — through every place it currently lives at Lerato's, and show how they drift apart.
  • Explain single source of truth: for each fact, exactly one place is authoritative, and everything else reads from it.
  • Make the subtle point that matters most: the problem is not the duplicate copies. It is that nobody can tell which copy is right, so staff stop trusting all of them and go back to asking the owner.
  • Use the shop's real example: six places hold a stock figure, all six disagree, so the stock figure is effectively unknown.

Four ways to join two systems

In rising order of cost and falling order of pain.

ApproachHow it worksCostsFails when
1. Re-entry
a person retypes		 Somebody reads one screen and types into another R0 upfront
hours every month, forever		 Immediately and invisibly. Typing errors, things done late, things skipped when it is busy.
2. File export and import
CSV between systems		 Export from one, import into the other, usually monthly Low
someone must remember		 The data is only as current as the last export, and nobody notices when the export stops working.
3. Built-in integration
the vendors already did it		 Two products that were built to connect, switched on in settings Subscription
little setup		 You are limited to what the vendors chose to connect, and tied to both of them.
4. Custom integration
an API and a developer		 Software written to move data between systems automatically Highest
plus ongoing maintenance		 The developer leaves, or one vendor changes their API and it quietly stops. This is the one that strands small businesses.
The recommendation for this shop

Prefer option 3 and design around it. For a four-person business, choosing two products that already integrate is almost always better than choosing two "better" products and paying somebody to join them. The joined version is only better until the person who built it is unavailable.

Write the integration approaches section (350–450 words — marked sub-topic)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain what an API is without jargon. A good analogy for a hardware shop: a trade counter. You do not walk into the warehouse; you hand a written order over the counter in an agreed format and get back what you asked for.
  • Explain real-time versus batch and why it matters here: a stock figure that updates monthly cannot support a reorder decision made today.
  • Work through the real integration challenges, not just the list: different systems meaning different things by the same word (is "customer" the person or the account?), different formats for dates and phone numbers, deciding which system wins when both have changed the same record, and what happens to data captured while the internet was down.
  • Cover vendor lock-in honestly: once a shop's three years of history is inside a product, moving becomes expensive and the vendor knows it. The defence is the data export test.
  • State the practical test to apply before signing anything: can I export everything, in a readable format, without asking permission? Link to the demonstration's export screen.

Evidence you can point at: The Your data screen exports every record with its metadata intact. Apply the same test to any product a vendor demonstrates.

Write the vendor selection section (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Frame it as a buying guide for somebody who is not technical and will be sold to confidently.
  • Name actual South African options you have verified — accounting, point of sale, payments — and say what each is genuinely good at. Check current pricing on the vendors' own sites and cite the date you checked, because it will have changed.
  • Cover the total cost of ownership idea: subscription, hardware, training time, the cost of the fortnight where everything is slower, and the cost of getting out again.
  • Be explicit that the team is not recommending one product. A consultant who names a product without understanding the client's constraints is selling, not advising.

Where to look it up: Vendors' own published pricing pages, with the date you checked. Do not cite a review site as a price.

What integration actually feels like

The thing nobody puts in the brochure

The hardest part of connecting two systems is almost never technical. It is discovering that the two halves of the business never agreed on what a word means. The counter thinks a "customer" is the person standing there; the bookkeeper thinks it is the account being billed. Both have been right for years. A system forces the question, and answering it is organisational work, not software work.

Write the implementation reality section (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Set honest expectations. An integration project of this size is weeks, not days, and most of the time goes on agreeing definitions and cleaning data rather than on configuration.
  • Explain data migration and the rule that matters: a migration imports whatever you give it. Migrating the old mess produces a new system full of the old mess, which the shop is now also paying for.
  • Describe parallel running — keeping the book going alongside the system for a few weeks — and the trade-off: more work for a fortnight, but a way back if it fails.
  • Describe what success and failure each look like three months in, concretely.

Evidence you can point at: Every record migrated from the old spreadsheet in the demonstration is marked unverified in its metadata. Explain why that honesty is deliberate — see the Data quality screen.
