Topic 5

Adapting a system to fit the shop

Every business believes it is unusual. Most are unusual in two or three ways that matter and ordinary in forty that do not. Telling those apart is most of the work — and getting it wrong is why systems get bought and then quietly abandoned.

Write the buy-configure-customise-build section (350–450 words — marked sub-topic)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Define each of the four clearly. The configure versus customise distinction is the important one and is routinely blurred by salespeople — configuration is changing settings the product offers; customisation is code written against the product.
  • Explain why customisation is where small businesses get hurt: every upgrade has to be retested, the person who wrote it moves on, and the shop now owns a software project it did not plan to own.
  • Make the recommendation and defend it. For a four-person shop: configure heavily, customise almost never, build only the one thing that is genuinely unique to the business.
  • Explain fit-gap analysis in plain terms: list what the shop needs, see what the product does, and for every gap decide to change the process, accept the gap, or pay to close it. Most gaps should be the first two.
  • Give the test for when building is justified: the thing is central to how this business competes, no product does it, and the shop can maintain it afterwards. Very few things pass all three.

Where to bend the shop, and where to bend the system

Before shopping, split what the shop does into two lists. Most of the argument during an implementation is really a disagreement about which list something belongs on.

Bend the shop

Done this way out of habit, not advantage. Changing it costs a week of grumbling and nothing else.

  • Invoice numbering and layout
  • How the product list is categorised
  • When the day is cashed up
  • Which report the bookkeeper gets, and in what format
  • Where a customer's details are captured in the conversation

Bend the system

How this shop actually competes. Force these to change and the shop loses something real.

  • Negotiated contractor prices agreed by handshake
  • Selling in part units — three metres of pipe, half a bag of sand
  • Letting a known customer take goods now and settle Friday
  • Trading through a power cut without turning customers away
  • Serving customers in Setswana, Sepedi, isiZulu, English and Afrikaans across one counter

Write the bend-the-shop versus bend-the-system section (300–400 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain how to tell the two lists apart. The test: does the customer notice, and would they go elsewhere if it changed?
  • Take the handshake pricing example seriously. It looks like untidy data and it is actually a competitive advantage in a market built on relationships. A system that forbids it will be bypassed within a week.
  • Take the offline requirement seriously. Load shedding is not an edge case in South Africa; it is Tuesday. A cloud-only point of sale that cannot take a sale during an outage fails at the exact moment the shop needs it. Say what the shop should require instead.
  • Cover the language point. Staff and customers move between languages mid-sentence. Software in English only is usable by staff but adds friction, and a customer-facing screen in English only sends a message.
  • Make the organisational point: this list is a business decision, not an IT one, and the owner must make it before the demonstrations start, not during them.

A worked example: the capture form

The demonstration's customer form is the argument of this page made concrete. It is generated from one file — data/schema.js — which describes what a customer record contains. Change that file and the form, the validation rules and the stored records all follow.

It adapts while you type

Choose "Business" and a VAT number field appears. Tick marketing consent and a date field appears, because POPIA now requires it. Choose cash-only and the credit limit disappears.

Asking a cash customer for a credit limit is inviting a wrong answer. The smallest correct form is the one that gets filled in properly.

It teaches at the right moment

Guidance appears next to the field it applies to, when it becomes relevant — not in a manual, and not in a training session three weeks before go-live.

Tick marketing consent and the POPIA note appears beside it, in one sentence.

It changes in one place

When the shop decides it needs to record something new — a delivery address, a trade category — that is one entry in the schema file.

Not a form change, plus a validation change, plus a database change, plus three places that were missed.

Write the adaptive interface section (300–400 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain progressive disclosure in plain language: show what is relevant now, reveal the rest when it becomes relevant. Then explain why it matters at a counter with a customer waiting.
  • Connect it to data quality. A shorter, more relevant form gets filled in properly; a long form with irrelevant fields gets filled with anything that lets the person move on. The interface design is a data quality control.
  • Explain schema-driven design without jargon: describe the thing once, and let the screen, the checks and the storage all read from that description. Say why that makes the system cheap to change — which is the whole topic of this page.
  • Cover accessibility and real conditions honestly: the screen is used standing up, on a counter, sometimes with dusty hands, by staff with varying levels of computer confidence. Big targets, clear errors, no jargon in messages.
  • Give the counter-example from the demonstration: the stock take sheet deliberately omits the system's own figure, because a counter who can see the expected number will tend to agree with it. Design decisions like that are what fitting a system to a business actually means.

Evidence you can point at: The customer form, and the stock take sheet under Documents.

The part that decides whether any of this works

Systems are not abandoned because they are bad

They are abandoned because on a busy Saturday the book is faster, and nobody ever explained what the extra thirty seconds buys. Staff adoption is not a training problem. It is a design problem and a management problem, and it is where most small-business IT spending is actually lost.

Write the user adoption section (300–400 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • Explain resistance fairly rather than dismissively. Staff resist for rational reasons: it is slower at first, it makes their mistakes visible, and nobody asked them.
  • Describe what the shop should actually do: involve Sipho and Thabo in choosing it, start with one thing that makes their day easier rather than the owner's, and let the person who learns it fastest teach the others.
  • Make the point about visibility honestly. A system does make individual performance visible, and that is genuinely uncomfortable. The owner should say out loud what that information will and will not be used for.
  • Explain why "make the right thing the easy thing" beats enforcement every time, with an example from the till screen.
  • Describe the failure signal to watch for: staff entering fiction to make the system accept a save. When that starts, the system is now producing worse data than the book it replaced, and the rule that caused it must change.

Designing for South African conditions

ConditionWhat it breaksWhat to require instead
Load shedding Cloud-only systems; anything that cannot take a sale offline Offline capture that syncs when power returns, on a device with its own battery
Patchy or expensive data Systems that re-download everything on every screen Works on a slow connection; does not need video; tolerates dropouts mid-sale
Cash-heavy trade Systems that assume every sale is carded and reconciled automatically Proper cash handling, a cash-up that matches the drawer, and a record of who counted
Part-unit selling Rigid product catalogues that only sell whole items Units of measure that include metres, kilograms and bags — and decimals in quantities
Multilingual counter Staff-facing screens and customer documents in English only At minimum, plain English on documents. Ask what the vendor supports; do not assume.
Staff turnover Systems only one person knows how to use Short written procedures, and more than one person who can do every task
SARS and POPIA Products built for another country's tax and privacy rules Correct VAT handling, compliant tax invoices, and a way to answer a POPIA request

Write the South African conditions section (250–350 words)

Cover, in your own words:

  • This section is where the Scenario Application marks are won. Keep every example specific to this country and this kind of business.
  • Lead with load shedding and treat it as a design requirement rather than a complaint. What does the shop do during stage 4 at 5pm on a Friday?
  • Cover the cost of data honestly, and what that means for choosing software.
  • Cover the cash point properly: a system that assumes card payments will not reconcile for a shop where a third of takings are cash.
  • Find and cite at least one credible South African source on small-business digital adoption or load shedding's impact on SMEs.

Where to look it up: Statistics South Africa, a reputable SME survey, or a South African banking or business-association report. Cite it properly.
