Data ethics, privacy and POPIA

The Protection of Personal Information Act applies to a hardware shop in Soshanguve exactly as it applies to a bank in Sandton. The difference is that the bank has a compliance department and the shop has Lerato.

Why this lands on a hardware shop at all

The Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) is applicable to Lerato's Hardware & Home because the store gathers and utilizes data about identifiable individuals. This personal information encompasses details like a customer's name, phone number, address, identity number, purchase history, and credit account balance. Processing involves any action taken with this information such as collecting it, recording it in a book or spreadsheet, altering it or sharing it. Consequently, even if Lerato relies on paper records rather than advanced software systems for record-keeping activities at present times; they are still considered processors under POPIA due solely based upon their handling methods concerning collected customer particulars.

The shop bears responsibility as it determines the purpose and manner of using customer information. The customer is referred to as the data subject, which means they are the individual to whom the information pertains. The Information Regulator acts as an independent entity charged with overseeing compliance with POPIA and addressing any related complaints.

A small business may contend that privacy laws are primarily designed for large corporations. Nonetheless, POPIA does not offer a blanket exemption based solely on the size of a business. The specific obligations vary depending on the type of information processed and associated risks; however, Lerato must still ensure legal processing of data, use it appropriately, and safeguard against loss, unauthorized access, or misuse.

The primary legal reason is that the store must adhere to the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013. The key sections of this Act took effect on July 1, 2020, with a general compliance deadline set for July 1, 2021. Compliance should not be considered optional by the store simply because it has a small number of employees.

There is also an ethical imperative to safeguard customer information. Lerato operates within a community where customers often include neighbors, contractors, and frequent shoppers. Unwarranted disclosure of details such as a customer's outstanding debt or purchase history could severely damage trust and reputation, with repercussions potentially more significant than immediate financial costs. Customers need assurance that their personal data is collected for legitimate purposes only and won't be used to embarrass, pressure, or exploit them in any way. Complying with POPIA not only fulfills legal obligations but also helps maintain fair and trustworthy relationships with customers.

POPIA sets forth eight conditions for lawful processing: accountability, limitation on processing, specification of purpose, limits on further processing, quality of information, transparency (openness), security measures in place to safeguard data and the involvement of the data subject. These conditions apply to Lerato’s business regardless if customer records are maintained in paper books or spreadsheets.

Data processing limitations require that personal information be handled lawfully and fairly, ensuring the collection of data is suitable, relevant, and not excessive for its intended purpose. To minimize privacy risks, it's best to avoid gathering unnecessary information from the outset. For instance, a cash customer purchasing a bag of cement typically doesn't need to give an identity number or home address. Conversely, when a contractor applies for credit with the shop, additional details may be needed so their account can be properly evaluated and managed. On Monday, Lerato could examine each form carefully and eliminate any fields lacking clear business or legal justification. This action aligns with sections 9–12 of POPIA.

The shop is obligated to implement security measures to safeguard personal information against loss, damage, unauthorized access, and unlawful use. For example, a spreadsheet containing customer names, phone numbers, and debts should not be accessible to all employees solely because it resides on the store's laptop. Lerato must limit access based on job roles by employing strong passwords; securing unattended devices; maintaining safe backups; and avoiding informal sharing of customer records. If there is any breach in security protocols or data protection obligations under Section 22 require notifying both the Information Regulator as well affected individuals consistent with guidelines outlined within relevant legislation The store needs a straightforward incident response strategy rather than waiting till major consequences arise Sections 19-22 offer insights into expectations regarding safeguarding procedures along with corresponding responsibilities.

Data subject participation grants customers the right to oversee their personal information, allowing them to request access and correct inaccuracies. Maintaining a handwritten book or disorganized spreadsheet complicates identifying all records associated with an individual. Lerato should ensure customer records are systematically organized and implement a procedure for receiving, verifying, and responding to such requests efficiently. For instance, if a customer inquiry about the personal details held by the shop, staff should be able to find these specific records quickly without sifting through unrelated documents endlessly. Sections 23–25 cover access rights and related participation entitlements.

These conditions demonstrate that compliance involves more than just having a privacy notice. It also relies on daily decisions regarding what the shop collects, who has access to this information, and how effectively the shop can respond when customers exercise their rights.

Question 1: What personal information does the shop hold about me?

Consider a scenario where a customer inquires at the counter on a Tuesday afternoon. The shop should be capable of quickly accessing their customer profile, including contact information, credit-account details, and relevant purchase history. A spreadsheet with scattered rows may not offer comprehensive answers since variations in name spelling or entries across multiple files might exist for the same individual. Implementing an organized record-keeping system facilitates identifying all pertinent information associated with one customer more efficiently. Section 23 of POPIA specifies guidelines concerning access to personal data.

Question 2: Why are you keeping my information?

The shop needs to clearly explain why it collects information using simple language. For instance, a phone number might be needed to notify a customer about their delivery, or financial details may be required for managing an approved credit account. The shop shouldn't collect information just on the off chance that it could be beneficial in the future. If there's ever a need to use this data for something new, the shop must evaluate whether this new purpose aligns with the original reason for collection or if it's legally justified under POPIA guidelines.

Question 3: Who can see my information, and has it been shared?

Lerato must be aware of which employees require access to customer details and if any information has been shared with an external party. For instance, while a cashier may need to process sales transactions, they do not necessarily need visibility into each customer's outstanding debts. Access should align with specific job responsibilities, and sharing information with suppliers for marketing purposes unrelated to business operations should not occur casually. Additionally, the shop needs to maintain transparency about its data processing practices.

Question 4: How long will you keep my information?

POPIA Section 14 typically restricts the retention of personal information beyond its necessary period, unless an exception is legally justified. Nonetheless, business records might need to be kept for tax-related purposes. SARS mandates that pertinent tax documentation should generally be preserved for a duration of five years in accordance with applicable regulations. Lerato should ensure essential invoices and transaction evidence are maintained while securely erasing or expunging any unnecessary personal details eligible for lawful removal. A request from a customer does not automatically necessitate the destruction of every legally required transaction record.

Under the definition provided in Section 1 of POPIA, consent must be given voluntarily, specifically, and with full knowledge. Simply remaining silent or using a pre-ticked box does not adequately demonstrate consent. Furthermore, Section 69 outlines particular guidelines for electronic direct marketing; thus, it's essential to ensure that consent is obtained based on the specific intent rather than considering it as a one-size-fits-all solution for different types of data processing activities. Implementing an organized process and maintaining clear records enable Lerato to respond accurately to customer inquiries while upholding their rights.

The honest point about the WhatsApp group

Customer details discussed in a staff WhatsApp group are personal information being processed on devices the shop does not control, with no retention, no access control and no way to answer a section 23 request. It is the most common and least noticed POPIA problem in South African small business, and no software fixes it. It is fixed by telling staff to stop.

Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling

Legal compliance and ethical behavior are related, yet distinct concepts. Compliance involves adhering to the law, while ethics concerns evaluating whether an action is fair, respectful, and responsible, even in situations not directly addressed by legal requirements. At Lerato's Hardware & Home, this distinction is crucial as customer relationships are personal, and the store relies on trust within its community.

Consider a customer with a history of frequently purchasing building materials and an increasing outstanding balance. Lerato might see this as an opportunity to extend additional credit to the customer. While the shop may view this offer as assistance, it’s important to recognize that the customer could be facing financial difficulties and might feel compelled to take on more debt than they can handle. An ethical approach involves responsibly assessing whether extending further credit is affordable for the customer, clearly explaining all terms involved, and refraining from exploiting private buying habits during vulnerable times. Just because a system flags someone as a potential candidate for more credit doesn’t mean that action should automatically follow without careful consideration.

Another issue involves staff access. Should every counter assistant have the ability to view their neighbor's purchase history and outstanding debt? While broad access might offer convenience, it also opens up unnecessary opportunities for gossip, embarrassment, and misuse of information. Staff should only be able to see information necessary for performing their responsibilities. Thus, implementing access controls is not just about a lack of trust in employees; it's also about minimizing temptation as well as preventing accidental disclosures and avoidable harm.

A third dilemma arises when a supplier offers a discount in return for the shop's customer list. While such disclosure might be legally permissible with adequate legal grounds and protections, it could still be unethical if customers did not reasonably anticipate that their information would be shared. Lerato must evaluate whether the potential business benefits outweigh losing customer trust and determine if the supplier truly requires identifiable customer details.

Three key principles can inform these decisions. Transparency ensures that customers are aware of what information the shop has and how it is used, preventing any surprises. Proportionality involves gathering only the necessary information for a legitimate purpose. Fairness takes into account the power imbalance between businesses and customers who might believe sharing their phone number is essential to securing a credit account.

In the end, Lerato should ask herself: "Would I feel at ease if the customer stood next to me and read this record alongside me?" If her answer is no, then the shop must reevaluate who has access to this information, why it is being utilized, and whether it's truly necessary. While POPIA provides a baseline standard for data protection, responsible business practices demand safeguarding both customers' dignity and their personal information.

What the shop has to do, in order

  1. Appoint and register the Information Officer

    In a shop this size it is the owner, automatically. Registration with the Information Regulator is a separate step. Verify the current registration process and whether it carries a fee, and cite the source.

  2. Write down what you hold, and why

    One page. Every category of personal information, the reason for it, and how long it is kept. This is the document the Regulator asks for first.

  3. Put a short notice at the counter

    Plain language: what you collect, why, who you share it with, and how to ask for it or have it removed. Six sentences beats six pages.

  4. Stop collecting what you do not need

    The fastest compliance win available, and it is free. Every field you do not collect is a field you cannot lose, misuse or be asked about.

  5. Fix the consent register

    Anyone you cannot prove consented should be asked again or taken off the list.

  6. Secure the device, then test the backup

    Password, screen lock, and a restore that somebody has actually performed. A backup that has never been restored is a hope.

  7. Tell the staff the WhatsApp rule

    Costs nothing, closes the largest hole, and needs saying more than once.

On penalties

POPIA carries administrative fines and, for the most serious offences, criminal penalties. The team must verify the current maximum amounts and the circumstances in which they apply directly from the Act and the Regulator, and cite the source. Do not quote a figure you have seen repeated on a blog — it is the kind of claim a client will check.
